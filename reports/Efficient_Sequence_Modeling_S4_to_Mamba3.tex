\title{Efficient Sequence Modeling: From S4D to \mamba{}-3\\
\large An Experimental Study of Five Sequence Architectures in Pure PyTorch}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Vaibhav Mahore}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Undergraduate Programme, Mathematics and Computing}\\ \textit{Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru, India}\\ mvaibhav@iisc.ac.in}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Quadratic attention cost limits Transformer-based sequence models at long
context, motivating a family of recurrent alternatives culminating in
selective state space models (SSMs) such as \mamba{}. This paper presents an
experimental study in which five sequence backbones: a causal Transformer,
S4D, \mamba{}-1, \mamba{}-2 (chunked State Space Duality), and
\mamba{}-3 (complex-valued transitions with exponential-trapezoidal
discretization) are implemented from first principles in pure PyTorch,
share one training skeleton, and are checked by equivalence tests that compare
independent computational forms of the same mathematics against each other.
All architectures are evaluated on three tasks probing different
capabilities (parity state tracking, selective copying, IMDb sentiment
classification) and benchmarked for latency, peak memory, and throughput at
sequence lengths from 1K to 32K on a single 6\,GB consumer GPU; only the
Transformer and S4D cover the whole range, and their 32K points exceeded device
memory and ran under Windows system-memory fallback. At small scale and with a
single seed, the results are consistent with capability claims of the
\mamba{}-3 literature without isolating their cause: \mamba{}-3, whose complex
transition rotates the state by an input-dependent angle, reaches 98.3\% token
accuracy on a cumulative-parity task where the real-transition \mamba{}-1/2,
the complex but LTI S4D, and the Transformer stay near chance. On selective
copying, LTI S4D scores 6\% token accuracy and the best selective SSM
(\mamba{}-2) 67\%, which is almost exactly what a model that copies only the
first five answers would score: the test asks for up to ten values, while
training never asks for more than five. In the efficiency sweep, the
Transformer's training step grows from 30\,ms at 1K tokens to 4.5\,s at 16K
(186\,s at 32K, a point that ran under system-memory fallback); the
FFT-convolution form of S4D, a much smaller model (182{,}784 parameters against
1{,}579{,}520 for the fp32 Transformer), is the fastest implementation in this
codebase at every measured length; and educational sequential scans are
$O(T)$ in operations but far slower in wall clock (2.6\,s versus 30\,ms per
step at 1K for \mamba{}-1 versus the Transformer), illustrating the gap between
asymptotic complexity and hardware behavior. All reported numbers come from
committed result files, but they were produced by an earlier revision of the
code with defects that have since been fixed (see the note before the
introduction); they have not been re-run and are stale. Scope restrictions
(S4D rather than full S4, a simplified \mamba{}-3 without MIMO or a parallel
form, no fused kernels) are stated.
\end{abstract}

figure in this paper was produced by an earlier revision of the code that
contained defects since fixed in the repository. In that revision the S4D
input matrix was computed as $\Delta B\,(e^{z}-1)$, without the division by
$z=\Delta A$ that zero-order hold requires; \mamba{}-2 and \mamba{}-3
expanded the shared $B$ and $C$ so that each head received only $N/H$ of their
$N$ entries, each repeated $H$ times; weight decay was applied to the
two-dimensional state-matrix parameters of S4D, \mamba{}-1 and \mamba{}-3
(for \mamba{}-3 including the rotation frequencies $\theta$); the \mamba{}-2
chunked scan took the logarithm of a clamped
$e^{\Delta A}$ instead of using $\Delta A$ directly; and the timed training
step of the efficiency sweep had no output projection. The experiments have
not been re-run with the fixed code, so all reported results are stale and
describe the pre-fix models. The provenance of each result file is documented
in \texttt{results/PROVENANCE.md} in the accompanying repository.

\section{Introduction}
Modeling dependencies over long sequences is a central requirement in
language, audio, and time-series applications. The dominant architecture,
the Transformer \cite{vaswani2017attention}, computes pairwise interactions
between all positions: self-attention costs $O(T^2)$ in compute for sequence
length $T$. Memory-efficient attention kernels reduce activation memory but
not the asymptotic compute, and the cost of long-context training and
inference remains substantial.

State space models (SSMs) offer an alternative: a linear recurrence over a
fixed-size hidden state with $O(T)$ total operations and constant per-token
state at inference \cite{gu2022efficiently,gu2021lssl}. The S4 family
introduced structured, HiPPO-initialized transition matrices that endow the
recurrence with long-range memory \cite{gu2020hippo}, computed either as a
recurrence or, for linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, as a convolution.
\mamba{} \cite{gu2023mamba} made the transition parameters input-dependent
(selectivity), \mamba{}-2 \cite{dao2024transformers} restricted the
transition to a scalar per head so that the recurrence can be evaluated as
chunked matrix products (State Space Duality, SSD), and \mamba{}-3
\cite{lahoti2026mamba3} restored expressivity needed for state tracking
through complex-valued transitions and a more accurate discretization rule.

This paper documents an experimental study built around five from-scratch
implementations of these architectures. The goals are threefold: (1) verify
that the mathematical claims of each architecture hold when the recurrence is
written out and tested, (2) measure which capabilities each architecture
actually exhibits on small controlled tasks, and (3) quantify the gap between
asymptotic complexity and wall-clock performance on consumer hardware. The
contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item Five sequence backbones (Transformer, S4D, \mamba{}-1, \mamba{}-2 with
a chunked SSD scan, \mamba{}-3 with complex transitions and
exponential-trapezoidal discretization) implemented in pure PyTorch behind a
single shared interface, with no dependency on fused SSM kernels.
\item Equivalence tests that compare independent computational forms of the
same mathematics (FFT-convolution versus recurrent stepping; chunked SSD
versus a sequential reference; \mamba{}-3 versus the \mamba{}-1/2 update in
its limiting configuration). They caught three implementation bugs during
development; a fourth surfaced only as a crash in the efficiency sweep, and two
defects that affect the reported numbers went undetected
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
\item A three-task capability study (cumulative-parity state tracking,
selective copying, IMDb sentiment) with shared data, optimizer and schedule
per task and one seed per configuration (parameter counts differ across
models), whose results are consistent with the capability claims of the
\mamba{}-3 and \mamba{} literature at small scale but do not isolate their
cause.
\item A sequence-length efficiency sweep (1K to 32K tokens; only the
Transformer and S4D cover the full range) measuring training-step latency,
peak memory, throughput, and decode cost on a 6\,GB RTX 4050 Laptop GPU, with
measured hardware limits recorded rather than omitted. Several points exceeded
device memory and ran under Windows system-memory fallback
(Table~\ref{tab:eff}).
\end{itemize}

Scope: the S4 implementation is the diagonal S4D restriction
\cite{gu2022s4d}, not full Normal-Plus-Low-Rank S4; \mamba{}-3 covers the
recurrence as implemented here (static rotation frequencies that the input
modulates only through $\Delta_t$, a real decay per head and state slot, real
$B$ and $C$ with a real-part readout), not its MIMO formulation or chunked
dual; scans are
educational, not kernel-fused. These restrictions are reflected throughout.

\section{Background: State Space Models}

\subsection{Continuous-Time Formulation}
A (diagonal) SSM maps a 1-D input $u(t)$ to an output $y(t)$ through a hidden
state $x(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ governed by
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}(t) = A\,x(t) + B\,u(t), \qquad y(t) = \mathrm{Re}\!\left(\sum_{n} C_n x_n(t)\right) + D\,u(t),
\label{eq:cont}
\end{equation}
where $A=\mathrm{diag}(A_1,\dots,A_N)$ holds the per-slot transition
parameters, $B$ injects the input, $C$ reads the state, and $D$ is a skip
connection. The real part of $A_n$ controls decay and the imaginary part
controls rotation frequency; stability requires
$\mathrm{Re}(A_n)<0$.

\subsection{Discretization}
Because tokens are discrete, the system is integrated with step size
$\Delta$. Under zero-order-hold (ZOH) discretization with diagonal $A$,
\begin{equation}
\bar{A}_n = e^{\Delta A_n}, \qquad
\bar{B}_n = \Delta B_n \,\phi(\Delta A_n), \quad \phi(z) = \frac{e^{z}-1}{z}.
\label{eq:zoh}
\end{equation}
The factor $\phi(z)$ tends to $1$ as $z\to 0$; evaluating it naively produces
a $0/0$ condition, so the implementation uses a Taylor fallback
$\phi(z)\approx 1+z/2+z^2/6$ for $|z|<10^{-4}$ and the closed form elsewhere.
The code that produced every S4D result in this paper omitted the division by
$z$ outside the Taylor branch, so it used $\bar{B}_n=\Delta B_n\,(e^{z}-1)$,
which differs from \eqref{eq:zoh} by the factor $z=\Delta A_n$. The current
code implements \eqref{eq:zoh} exactly and tests it against a
matrix-exponential reference, but the S4D numbers reported below were not
regenerated.
The discretized system is the linear recurrence
\begin{equation}
x_t = \bar{A}\,x_{t-1} + \bar{B}\,u_t .
\label{eq:disc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Convolution and Recurrence: Two Forms of One System}
Unrolling \eqref{eq:disc} for an LTI system yields $y = K * u$ with kernel
\begin{equation}
K_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} C_n \bar{B}_n \bar{A}_n^{\,k}, \qquad
\bar{A}_n^{\,k} = e^{k\Delta A_n}.
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
Because $\bar{A}_n^k$ is an exponential in $k$ (a Vandermonde structure), the
kernel is computed without iterating powers, and the training-time output is
a causal FFT convolution computed in $O(T\log T)$. At inference, the same
system advances with $O(1)$ work per token using
\eqref{eq:disc} directly. The two forms are mathematically identical, a
property our test suite exploits (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

\subsection{S4D}
The S4D restriction \cite{gu2022s4d} keeps a complex diagonal transition
$A_n=-\exp(a_n) + i\pi n$ (S4D-Lin initialization), a per-channel step size
$\Delta$, the softplus of a learned parameter, initialized log-spaced across
channels in $[10^{-3},10^{-1}]$ and only constrained to stay positive during
training, and complex
input/output vectors $B,C$ initialized at scale $1/\sqrt{N}$. Training uses
the FFT convolution of \eqref{eq:kernel} with a chunked kernel computation
(chunks of 1024 positions); decode uses a complex state of shape
$(B,D,N)$ updated once per token. The block applies a SiLU gate on a second
linear branch, so although the recurrence is LTI the block is not.

\subsection{\mamba{}-1: Selectivity}
\mamba{} \cite{gu2023mamba} makes $\Delta_t$, $B_t$, and $C_t$ functions of
the input through a linear projection, breaking the LTI assumption and with
it the convolution form: the system becomes time-varying and training
requires a scan. With $\alpha_t = e^{\Delta_t A}\in(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
x_t = \alpha_t\, x_{t-1} + \Delta_t B_t\, u_t .
\label{eq:selective}
\end{equation}
The block is: input projection to two branches, a causal short convolution
(kernel 4), SiLU activation, the selective scan, a SiLU gate, and an output
projection under a pre-norm residual. The $\Delta$ projection bias is
initialized so that $\Delta$ starts log-spaced across channels in
$[10^{-3},10^{-1}]$, so that different channels operate at different
timescales. Decode maintains both the SSM state
and the convolution history, one token at a time.

\subsection{\mamba{}-2: State Space Duality}
\mamba{}-2 \cite{dao2024transformers} restricts the transition to a scalar
per head, $A = a_h\,\mathbb{I}$. The unrolled recurrence then factors into a
lower-triangular decayed sum that resembles attention:
\begin{equation}
Y = \bigl(L \odot (C B^{\top})\bigr) X + \text{carry-in}, \qquad
L_{ts} = \prod_{r=s+1}^{t}\alpha_r = e^{cs_t - cs_s},
\label{eq:ssd}
\end{equation}
where $cs$ is the cumulative sum of $\log\alpha$ and $L$ is masked to the
lower triangle. The sequence is processed in chunks of size $C$: within a
chunk, outputs are computed as decayed, masked matmuls; across chunks, a
state $S\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}$ is carried with
$S \leftarrow \alpha^{\mathrm{tot}} S + \sum_t \alpha^{\mathrm{suf}}_t\,
B_t\otimes x_t$. This reduces the sequential loop length by a factor of $C$
and turns the inner computation into matrix multiplications that can run on
tensor cores, which is the practical reason \mamba{}-2 exists. Here they run
in fp32 without autocast or fused kernels, and the efficiency sweep
(Section~VI-D) finds this form slower than the Transformer at every length it
completed. Our implementation processes chunks in
groups to bound transient memory. $B_t$ and $C_t$ are shared by all heads of a
group (one group in every configuration here). In the code behind the reported
results this expansion was wrong: each head read only $N/H$ of the $N$ entries
of $B_t$ and $C_t$, each repeated $H$ times, which for \mamba{}-2, whose decay
is a scalar per head, reduces the effective state per head to $N/H$. The same
code computed $\log\alpha_t$ as the logarithm of a clamped $e^{\Delta_t a_h}$,
which loses the gradient where the decay underflows. Both are fixed (every
head now reads its group's full vectors, and $\log\alpha_t=\Delta_t a_h$ is used
directly), but the \mamba{}-2 and \mamba{}-3 numbers below predate the fixes.

\subsection{\mamba{}-3: Complex Transitions and a Better Rule}
\mamba{}-3 \cite{lahoti2026mamba3} addresses a capability gap: transitions
with real non-negative eigenvalues can only decay state; they cannot rotate
it, which formally limits state tracking. The implemented recurrence adds
two of the three paper contributions. First, exponential-trapezoidal
discretization approximates the state-input integral with a data-dependent
combination of both endpoints,
\begin{equation}
x_t = \alpha_t x_{t-1} + \underbrace{(1-\lambda_t)\,\Delta_t \alpha_t\, B_{t-1} u_{t-1}}_{\beta_t} + \underbrace{\lambda_t \Delta_t}_{\gamma_t} B_t u_t,
\label{eq:trap}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_t=\sigma(\cdot)\in(0,1)$; setting $\lambda_t=1$ recovers the
exponential-Euler rule of \mamba{}-1/2, and the rule is second-order
accurate. Second, the transition becomes complex, $A=-\exp(a)+i\theta$: the
magnitude $|\alpha_t| = e^{-\Delta_t \exp(a)}<1$ guarantees stability
independent of phase, while the phase $\Delta_t\theta$ rotates the state. Here
$\theta$ is a learned but static parameter per head and state slot, so the
rotation angle depends on the input only through $\Delta_t$. Other details of
this implementation: $a$ is a separate parameter per head and state slot
(\mamba{}-2 uses one scalar per head), $B$ and $C$ are real and the readout
uses only $\mathrm{Re}(x_t)$, and neither the paper's MIMO formulation nor its
parallel (dual) form is implemented, so training runs as a sequential loop
over time.

\subsection{Transformer Baseline}
The baseline is a pre-norm causal Transformer using
\texttt{scaled\_dot\_product\_attention} in fp32, so PyTorch can use its
memory-efficient attention kernel but not the flash-attention kernel, with
absolute sinusoidal positions added once to the embeddings. Positions seen
only up to the training length may not carry over to longer sequences, which
matters for selective copying, where test sequences are twice as long as
training sequences. No KV cache is implemented. The current benchmark times a
full-prefix recomputation per new token, but the committed Transformer decode
values were recorded with an earlier definition (forward time divided by $T$),
so they understate the per-token cost of recomputation.

\section{Implementation and Verification}
\label{sec:impl}
The package (pure PyTorch, no SSM-kernel dependencies) exposes all
backbones behind one registry. Correctness is checked by equivalence tests
that compare independent implementations of the same mathematics:
the S4D FFT-convolution path against its recurrent step path; the
\mamba{}-2 chunked SSD against a sequential reference scan (chunk sizes 32
and 64, including a padded sequence); \mamba{}-3 against the
\mamba{}-1/2 recurrence at $\lambda_t=1$, $\theta=0$; and \mamba{}-1's
incremental decode against its full-sequence forward, which is itself a
sequential scan. Agreement is checked at absolute and relative tolerance
$10^{-3}$, appropriate for fp32 reassociation. Three development bugs were
caught by these tests (a conjugation-convention mismatch in S4D, a
triangular-mask construction that silently masked nothing, and a conv-state
slice on the wrong axis). A fourth, a kernel-chunk concatenation along the
wrong axis, was not: the S4D test ran at $T{=}64$, below the 1024-position
kernel chunk, and the bug surfaced as a crash in the efficiency sweep at
$T{=}2048$ (recorded in \texttt{experiments.log}). The tests also missed two
defects present when the reported results were produced, because the compared
forms either shared or bypassed the faulty code: the S4D convolution and step
paths call the same discretization function, and the \mamba{}-2 test fed
per-head $B$ and $C$ directly to the scans, bypassing the head expansion. Both
defects are now fixed, and the suite now also checks the ZOH factor against a
matrix-exponential reference, the S4D kernel across several chunks
($T{=}2100$), and the \mamba{}-2 and \mamba{}-3 blocks against naive per-head
references (for \mamba{}-3 with $\theta\neq 0$ and $\lambda_t\in(0,1)$). The
suite contains 152 tests covering the equivalence and reference
checks, masking, determinism, and dataset contracts.

\section{Experimental Setup}
Three tasks probe different capabilities; all models train under identical
protocols per task (shared data, AdamW optimizer, cosine schedule, and seed
42; one seed per configuration). Width and depth are matched per task, but
parameter counts are not (Tables~\ref{tab:parity} and~\ref{tab:imdb}).

\textbf{Parity (state tracking).} The target at position $t$ is the XOR of
inputs $1..t$; 2{,}000 training and 1{,}000 test sequences, $L=128$ for both
(so no length extrapolation is tested), 20 epochs. Following
\cite{lahoti2026mamba3}, a diagonal linear recurrence can track parity only if
its transition can flip or rotate the state depending on the input.

\textbf{Selective copying.} Marker-value pairs are embedded in a noise
stream; a separator then requests the $k$ remembered values in order. Train
$L=64$ (3 to 5 pairs), test $L=128$ (5 to 10 pairs), 12 epochs. Only $k$
values must be retained (constant in $L$) while noise must be gated out. The
test changes both the length and the number of values to recall (up to 10,
against at most 5 in training), so test scores mix recall with extrapolation
in $k$; no in-distribution score is reported.

\textbf{IMDb sentiment.} Standard binary classification, 12.5k training
subset (fixed seed), maximum length 512, 3 epochs, $d_{\text{model}}=128$,
4 blocks. Sequential-loop models (\mamba{}-1/3) are excluded from IMDb on
wall-clock grounds (Section~VI-D); the selective-SSM family is represented
by \mamba{}-2, which shares their input-dependent (selective) parameterization
but uses a real scalar decay per head. Scores are on the full 25k IMDb test
split, which also served as the per-epoch validation set; the reported values
are from the final epoch, not the best one.

\textbf{Efficiency sweep.} Training-step latency (forward, backward, update;
CUDA events, 5 repeats after 2 warm-up steps), peak memory, throughput, and
decode cost at $T\in\{1\mathrm{K},\dots,32\mathrm{K}\}$, batch 8,
$d_{\text{model}}=256$, 2 blocks, fp32 without autocast, on an RTX 4050 Laptop
GPU (6{,}140.5\,MB of device memory, Windows/WDDM). Memory is the peak
allocated by PyTorch, in MiB written as MB; reserved memory was not recorded.
Points whose peak allocation exceeds device memory could run only through the
WDDM system-memory fallback, so their times include paging over PCIe. In the
code that produced these numbers the timed training step had no output
projection: the backbone's 256-dimensional features were used directly as
256-way logits against targets drawn from a 32-token vocabulary. \mamba{}-1 and
\mamba{}-3 were capped at 4K by configuration, and the result file was
assembled from several restarted runs across code revisions.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Parity: Only \mamba{}-3 Tracks the State}
Table~\ref{tab:parity} and Fig.~\ref{fig:parity} show the study's headline
capability result. \mamba{}-3 reaches 98.3\% token accuracy (42\% of test
sequences fully correct) while \mamba{}-1, \mamba{}-2, S4D, and the
Transformer remain at 50.6\% to 56.3\% token accuracy with zero fully-correct
sequences. The likely mechanism is the rotation argument of
\cite{lahoti2026mamba3}: parity can be tracked with
$x_t = R(\pi u_t)\,x_{t-1}$, a rotation by an input-dependent angle. Real
non-negative transitions cannot rotate; S4D is complex but LTI, so its
rotation angle cannot depend on the input; \mamba{}-3's complex transition
combines stability ($|\alpha_t|<1$) with a phase $\Delta_t\theta$ that depends
on the input through $\Delta_t$. The result is consistent with the paper's
motivating claim but does not isolate the complex transition: \mamba{}-3 here
also differs from \mamba{}-2 in its per-slot decay, the trapezoidal $\beta_t$
term and the $\lambda_t$ gate, and no variant with $\theta=0$ or
$\lambda_t=1$ was trained. It is a single run (seed 42) with train and test
length 128; the \mamba{}-3 test accuracy dipped to 0.4802 at epoch 10 of 20
before recovering, and \mamba{}-1/2 end with test loss 0.620 and 0.607, below
$\ln 2$, so they are near chance rather than exactly at it.

\subsection{Selective Copying: \mamba{}-1/2 Lead, LTI S4D Near Chance}
On selective copying (Fig.~\ref{fig:selcopy}), \mamba{}-2 and \mamba{}-1 lead
with 0.670 and 0.649 token accuracy (18.3\% and 14.9\% full-sequence success
at test length 128), against Transformer 0.205 and S4D 0.064. These are
out-of-distribution scores, since test sequences hold 5 to 10 pairs and
training sequences 3 to 5. On this test set, a model that returns the first
five values correctly and fails on the rest would reach almost exactly
\mamba{}-2's token accuracy and a similar full-sequence success, so the result
is consistent with the selective SSMs having learned recall up to the training
pair count without extrapolating beyond it (an inference: per-$k$ accuracy was
not recorded). Training loss below $5\times10^{-4}$ against test loss of 1.20
(\mamba{}-2) and 2.41 (\mamba{}-1) points the same way. S4D stays near
chance, consistent with the argument of \cite{gu2023mamba} that an LTI kernel
cannot gate noise out of its state. The Transformer's absolute sinusoidal
positions were seen only up to length 64 in training, which may contribute to
its low score at length 128 (not tested). \mamba{}-3, also a selective model,
reaches only 0.215 token accuracy; no ablation explains this gap, and the run
predates the $B/C$ expansion fix.

\subsection{IMDb Sentiment}
With the same width, depth and schedule on real text (Table~\ref{tab:imdb},

\textbf{(a) The Transformer's step time grows superlinearly.} Up to 16K,
where its peak allocation (4{,}989\,MB) stays below device memory, the
Transformer's step time grows from 30\,ms at $T{=}1$K to 4{,}482\,ms. The 32K
point (186\,s, peak 9{,}879\,MB) exceeded device memory and ran under WDDM
system-memory fallback; its jump from 16K is far larger than the fourfold
increase a quadratic term alone predicts, so it mostly reflects paging and
should not be read as the cost of attention (a hypothesis; the two effects
were not separated). Reserved memory was not recorded, so it is not known
whether the 16K point stayed entirely in device memory. Only fp32 SDPA was
measured; no other attention kernel was compared.

\textbf{(b) FFT convolution is the fastest form measured here.} S4D has the
lowest step time at every measured length (13.5\,s per step at 32K, a point
that also exceeded device memory), consistent with a single FFT pair per layer
versus scans or matmul chains. The comparison is not size-matched: S4D has
182{,}784 parameters and no MLP, against 1{,}579{,}520 for the Transformer,
whose fp32 attention cannot use the flash kernel; a parameter-matched or bf16
comparison was not run. The chunked SSD form of \mamba{}-2 is far faster than
the sequential \mamba{}-1 scan (78.5\,ms versus 2.6\,s at 1K) but slower than
the Transformer at every length it completed (78.5 versus 30.1\,ms at 1K,
8{,}954 versus 847\,ms at 8K), so this sweep does not show an advantage of SSD
over attention at these lengths.

\textbf{(c) $O(T)$ operations can still lose.} The educational sequential
scans perform $O(T)$ work but are far slower than the vectorized forms at
equal length: 2.6\,s versus 30\,ms per step at $T{=}1$K for \mamba{}-1 versus
the Transformer, and 10.9\,s for \mamba{}-3, whose 1K point already exceeded
device memory. Each of the $T$ steps in every layer launches small kernels and
adds nodes to the autograd graph. This is precisely why fused
selective-scan kernels and the chunked SSD matmul form exist, and it is the
study's central complexity-versus-hardware lesson: asymptotics describe how
work grows, packaging determines how fast it runs.

\section{Discussion}
The tasks give a consistent, if preliminary, capability picture. Parity
suggests that the transition parameterization (complex, with input-dependent
phase) is a first-order architectural capability rather than an
implementation detail, although no ablation isolates it; selective copying
shows LTI S4D near chance, as expected for a model that cannot gate its
input, while the selective models' scores are consistent with recall only up
to the training pair count; IMDb shows small differences on natural language,
with the SSD-family model competitive end to end. All of these rest on
single-seed runs of the pre-fix code. The efficiency sweep separates these
capability questions from the deployment question; among the implementations
measured here the S4D convolution form was fastest, and fused kernels were not
measured.

\section{Limitations}
(1) The implementations are educational: python-loop scans are much slower in
wall clock than the vectorized forms in this codebase (Section~VI-D), and no
comparison against the official fused \mamba{} kernels (Linux-only) was run on
this machine, so their speed-up over these scans is not measured here. (2) Full S4
(NPLR parameterization with Woodbury-corrected Cauchy kernels) is not
implemented; S4D retains the ideas this study needs. (3) \mamba{}-3's MIMO
formulation and chunked dual are not implemented; its recurrence is, with
static rotation frequencies, a real decay per head and state slot, and real
$B$ and $C$ (Section~III-D).
(4) Task results use a single seed (42) and models of different parameter
counts; parity is tested only at its training length, and the selective-copy
test asks for more values than training does; IMDb uses a half-data subset and
512-token truncation. (5) The efficiency sweep is fp32, so attention cannot use
the flash kernel, and bf16 autocast would change absolute numbers; the timed
step had no output projection; and the result file was assembled from several
restarted runs across code revisions. (6) \mamba{}-3's sequential training
already exceeded device memory at $T{=}1$K (peak 6{,}646.7\,MB, run under
system-memory fallback) and ran out of memory at 2K; \mamba{}-1 stays below
device memory at 2K (peak 3{,}970.9\,MB), exceeded it at 4K (7{,}912.6\,MB,
again under fallback), and was capped at 4K by configuration. Their training
cost at longer lengths is therefore not reported and not extrapolated. (7)
Every number in this paper comes from code that predates the fixes described
before the introduction and is stale.

\section{Conclusion}
Five sequence architectures were implemented from first principles behind
one interface, checked by equivalence tests, and evaluated on three capability
tasks and an efficiency sweep from 1K to 32K tokens. The results come from one
seed and from code that predates the fixes described before the introduction,
so they are stale. Within those limits they are consistent with the main
capability claims of the recent SSM literature without isolating their causes:
\mamba{}-3, whose complex transition rotates by an input-dependent angle,
reached 98.3\% token accuracy on parity where \mamba{}-1/2, S4D and the
Transformer stayed near chance; the selective \mamba{}-1/2 beat LTI S4D on
selective copying (67\% versus 6\% token accuracy for the best selective
model), although 67\% is almost exactly what copying only the first five
answers would give; and the SSD model was competitive on IMDb. In the
efficiency sweep, the Transformer's step time grew superlinearly with length,
the FFT convolution of a much smaller S4D was the fastest implementation
measured, and the $O(T)$ loops were the slowest, a reminder that $O(T)$
recursion is only as fast as its packaging; the longest point of every model
exceeded device memory and includes paging. Code, configs, committed result
files, and the script that draws every figure, including those in this paper,
from the committed results are available in the accompanying
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/vaibhav3000/s4-to-mamba}}
